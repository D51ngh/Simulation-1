Simulation Lab: you set the noise and the timing

This is your playground. One helper qubit (the ancilla) checks four data qubits, drawn as Bloch spheres: Q1 at the top, Q2 on the right, Q3 at the bottom and Q4 on the left. On the left you decide what the noise does and how fast the circuit runs. Then play a single run to watch it happen, or run as many runs as you like to see the overall picture.

How to read this: each orange line shows how hard the noise is pushing that qubit over the whole run. The short blue marks are the moments the helper "looks at" that qubit, once per round. "flip" means a check turned that qubit's tilt into a full flip, which the decoder never sees directly. The dots along the top mark the rounds where the helper raised the alarm.

Each shot repeats the whole 8-round run with the same settings but fresh random luck. More shots give steadier numbers but take a little longer.

What's going on under the hood

  • Every nanosecond, each data qubit picks up a little phase, δω·dt, where δω is how hard the noise is pushing that qubit right then.
  • The helper "looks at" a qubit at the end of that qubit's CNOT. Any phase picked up after that moment has to wait for the next round.
  • When the readout finishes, each qubit's tilt θ becomes either nothing or a full flip, with chance sin²(θ/2) of a flip, just like in Step 3 of the tour. Either way, the tilt is wiped clean.
  • The helper only reports whether an odd or even number of its qubits flipped. So two flips in the same round cancel out, and a burst can slip by unseen.
  • An alarm (a detection event) goes off when the helper's answer changes from the last round. The latency is the time from when the noise started to when that alarm reaches the electronics.
  • One honest simplification: each data qubit is treated on its own. That gets the timing right, but a real surface code keeps the four qubits in one shared, entangled state.
