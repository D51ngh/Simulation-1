From phase noise to error correction

Quantum computers make mistakes all the time. This tour shows you, step by step and with your own hands, how one kind of mistake happens on a superconducting qubit, how the computer notices it, and how it fixes it.

Step 1

Meet the qubit and its phase

Let's start with a picture you'll use for the whole tour: the Bloch sphere. Every state of a qubit is an arrow pointing from the centre of this sphere to its surface.

At the top (north pole) sits |0⟩ and at the bottom sits |1⟩, just like the 0 and 1 of an ordinary bit. Around the middle, on the equator, are two special states we'll care about a lot: |+⟩, which we'll call "fine", and directly opposite it |−⟩, which we'll call "flipped". Our qubit starts at |+⟩.

Here's the surprising part: a real qubit never sits still. A transmon (a tiny superconducting circuit) has a natural frequency, so its arrow keeps spinning around the equator, like a hand on a clock. That spin is not a mistake. We know its speed exactly, so we can always predict where the arrow should be.

The trouble starts when the environment (stray magnetic fields, charges, defects in the chip) nudges that frequency by a tiny amount, called δω. Now the qubit spins slightly too fast or too slow, and slowly drifts away from where it should be. That drift angle is the phase error θ:

θ = ∫ δω(t) dt  ·  in words: the frequency mistake, added up over time

Physicists like to "ride along" with the correct spin, the way you'd stand on a merry-go-round to watch a friend on it. In that view, called the rotating frame, the correct arrow stands still and only the mistake moves. That's the view on the right below.

How we describe the noise

To study noise carefully we need a model we can control. We use the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process: noise that wanders randomly but "remembers" its recent past. It has just two knobs. The correlation time τc says how long the noise remembers itself: a long τc means slow, drifting noise, a short τc means fast, jittery noise. The strength V says how big the wobbles are. In this research we keep V fixed and turn τc, so any change we see comes from the noise's memory alone.

Remember: noise changes how fast the qubit spins. Added up over time, that makes the arrow drift away from where it should be by an angle θ, the phase error.

Step 2

A tilted qubit is part "fine" and part "flipped"

This is the most important idea of the whole tour, so let's go slowly.

When the arrow has drifted by some angle θ, you might think it's in a new, third kind of state. It isn't. A tilted qubit is exactly the same thing as a mixture of the fine state |+⟩ and the flipped state |−⟩. It's one state, written two ways, a bit like saying a direction is "10° east of north" or "mostly north, a little east".

tilted qubit = cos(θ/2) · |+⟩ − i · sin(θ/2) · |−⟩

The two numbers in front tell you how much of each piece is there. Square them and you get probabilities: if you check the qubit, it says "fine" with chance cos²(θ/2) and "flipped" with chance sin²(θ/2). Move the slider below and watch the two bars trade places.

Step 3

The check, one move at a time

We can't simply look at our qubit, because looking destroys the information stored in it. Instead we use a helper qubit, called the ancilla, as a go-between. One full check is called a round.

Here is what happens in one round, in plain words:

  1. Get the helper ready. Reset the ancilla to |0⟩, then a Hadamard gate turns it into |+⟩.
  2. Connect the two with a CNOT gate. This is the clever bit. The CNOT works like a label maker: the "fine" part of our qubit puts the label 0 on the ancilla, and the "flipped" part puts the label 1.
  3. Read the label. A Hadamard and a measurement read the ancilla. A measurement always gives one clear answer, 0 or 1, never "mostly 0".
  4. The tilt disappears. Whichever label came up, the part of the qubit with the other label is gone. Our qubit is now exactly fine or exactly flipped. Nothing rotated it back; the tilt simply decided the odds and then vanished.

The answer of each round is called the syndrome (0 = fine, 1 = flipped). What we really watch, though, is when that answer changes from one round to the next. That's called a detection event, and it's the only thing the error-correcting software ever gets to see.

1.0×

One shot can't tell you much. Many shots can.

A shot is one complete run of the experiment, here 8 rounds long. In one shot, each round only gives a yes or a no, so you can't tell whether the qubit drifted by 10° or by 60°. But if you repeat the same experiment many times and count how often each round fires, that fraction f is an estimate of sin²(θ/2). Undo the formula, θ = 2·arcsin√f, and you've recovered how much phase built up in that round. Choose your own number of shots above and see how close the blue row gets to the hidden grey row.

Try this
  • Run just 50 shots, then 20,000. With few shots the recovered angles jump around; with many they settle on the truth.
  • Notice what you can't recover: whether the qubit spun too fast or too slow. The sign is lost.
Remember: the CNOT labels the "fine" and "flipped" parts, reading the label keeps one and erases the tilt, and detection events record when the answer changed. Many shots together let you work back to the hidden phase.

Step 4

Checking often: why phase doesn't pile up

Many people expect continuous noise to keep pushing the qubit further and further away. It does keep acting, but every check wipes the tilt back to zero.

So the qubit only ever carries the phase it picked up since its last check. And because the chance of a flip grows with the square of the tilt, chopping the same total phase into many small, separately checked pieces makes flips much rarer. This has a lovely name: the quantum Zeno effect, after the ancient puzzle about the arrow that never arrives.

Step 5

Four qubits, one helper, and a strict timetable

In a real surface code, each helper qubit checks four data qubits at once. It talks to them one after another, on a fixed clock, every single round.

Here's the timetable we'll use. The numbers are made-up but realistic:

Here's the catch: the helper only "looks at" each qubit during that qubit's own CNOT. If noise hits a qubit just after its CNOT, this round has already looked away, and the problem only shows up next round. Choose a qubit and a time below, press Play, and watch it happen.

  • Move the hit to 170 ns. Now Q1 is caught in round 1. Ten nanoseconds made a whole round of difference!

This is called the gate-scheduling effect, and it matters a lot for this research. Two errors that happen at the very same moment can show up in different rounds, which can trick a decoder into thinking the noise travelled from one qubit to another when it didn't.

Step 6

The decoder: playing detective

Now let's zoom out to a small but complete error-correcting code: the distance-3 surface code. It protects one precious qubit by spreading it across nine data qubits, Q1 to Q9.

The purple patches are the checks that catch phase flips. When some of them fire, the decoder has to play detective: which qubits most likely flipped? It only sees which checks fired, never the errors themselves.

The standard detective is called minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM). Its idea is simple and sensible: errors are rare, so the best guess is the explanation that needs the fewest errors. Every data qubit is a link between the checks it touches. Each fired check has to be paired with another fired check, or with an edge of the code, and MWPM picks the pairing that crosses the fewest links. The fix is usually just written down in software (the Pauli frame) and applied when the qubits are finally read.

What real decoders do. On real hardware the checks repeat round after round, so the decoder matches in space and time: detection events from different rounds are clues too, and links also join rounds (for measurement mistakes and for errors delayed by the timetable from Step 5). Standard MWPM treats every link as equally likely, assuming errors are independent. Teaching it about timing and noise memory is where this research comes in.

Remember: the decoder only sees which checks fired, chooses the explanation with the fewest errors, and keeps the fix in software. It fails when the real errors happen to be less likely than some other explanation.

Step 7

The big picture: how much of the noise's memory reaches the decoder?

You've now seen every link in the chain. The research question is about what survives along that chain, and whether a decoder can use it.

Three kinds of memory

It helps to keep three "memories" apart, because they don't have to be the same:

NameIn plain wordsWhere you met it
Noise memory (τc)How long the noise remembers itselfStep 1
Qubit memoryHow long that memory stays in the qubit's phaseSteps 1 and 2
Syndrome memoryHow much memory is left in the detection record, after the checks keep wiping the tiltSteps 3 to 5

The decoder never sees the noise itself, only the detection record. So what really matters for the decoder is the syndrome memory, not the raw τc. Measuring how one turns into the other comes first.

To keep things understandable, we add one ingredient at a time. If something surprising happens, we know exactly which ingredient caused it.

Things that can fool us

A late detection doesn't always mean the noise arrived late. The timetable (Step 5), faulty measurements, slow build-up of phase (Step 4), one error lighting up two checks, and two errors cancelling each other can all make the record look like noise is spreading when it isn't. That's why the simulator always keeps the hidden truth (when the noise started, how much phase built up, which qubits really flipped) separate from what a decoder sees, so every guess can be checked against the real answer.

The research in one sentence: how much of the noise's memory survives through the qubit and the checks into the detection record, and can a decoder use it to fix errors better?
